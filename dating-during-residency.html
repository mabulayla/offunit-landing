Dating in residency

Dating during residency, when your schedule changes every rotation

You finally have a paycheck and a title, and somehow less free time than you had in med school. Dating during residency is possible. Plenty of residents meet someone, and plenty of relationships survive it. It just works better when you plan for how residency actually runs instead of how dating usually runs.

Why residency is its own kind of hard

Lots of jobs have long hours. Residency stacks a few things on top of each other that make dating harder than the hours alone suggest.

  • The hours are high, and the cap is an average. In the US, accreditation rules limit residents to 80 hours a week averaged over four weeks, with one day in seven free of duty, also averaged. That means some weeks are lighter and some are well over 80 in practice.
  • Your schedule changes every block. A month of ICU nights, then a month of outpatient clinic, then a surgery rotation with 5am pre-rounding. The pattern you worked out with someone in March may not exist in April.
  • Post-call is not a day off. After a long call shift you're technically free, but mostly you're asleep. Anyone who doesn't know medicine will see "off Tuesday" and assume you can do dinner.
  • You don't control much of it. Chief residents and program coordinators build the schedule. Swaps are possible in a lot of programs, but they cost favors.
  • You live where you matched. You may have moved somewhere you'd never picked, away from your friends, and you may move again for fellowship in a few years.
  • There's always something to study. In-training exams, boards, reading for tomorrow's cases. Free evenings come with a little guilt attached.

Use the time you actually have

Know your lighter rotations

Most programs have months that are easier than others: electives, clinic blocks, research time. Those are the best stretches to start dating. Starting something new in the middle of your hardest rotation sets both of you up for frustration.

Treat a golden weekend like gold

A full weekend off doesn't come around often for many residents. If you're dating someone, put one of those days aside for them before it fills up with errands and sleep.

Don't schedule dates post-call

You'll be tired, foggy and short on patience. Plan for the day after, or for an evening on a lighter day. If you have to meet post-call, keep it to a short coffee near home.

Small dates count

Lunch near the hospital on a clinic day, a walk on your one free afternoon, breakfast after a night shift. In residency, a 45-minute plan you actually keep is worth more than a big plan you cancel.

Be honest about what you can offer right now

  • Say it early. Mention that you're a resident, what specialty, and roughly what that means for your hours in the first few messages. "I'm on ICU nights this month, so I'm slow to reply in the afternoon" saves a lot of second-guessing.
  • Send the schedule when it's posted. Once you're past the first couple of dates, sharing your next block lets the other person plan too. It also shows you're making room for them.
  • Pick consistency over grand gestures. A short message at roughly the same time every day goes further than a big weekend once a month and silence in between.
  • Know what you want. Some residents want something serious and steady. Others know they don't have room for that until PGY-3. Either is fine. Saying which one it is, kindly, is the fair thing to do.
  • Don't promise what the schedule can't deliver. "I'll try to make it" is better than "I'll be there" when you're on call that night.

Dating someone in medicine vs outside it

Someone in medicine or healthcare

The big advantage is that you don't have to explain call, post-call or why you fell asleep mid-sentence. Another resident, a nurse or a pharmacist knows what a bad night on call looks like. If you're both in training, though, two unpredictable schedules can leave you with very little overlap, and someone has to bend.

If you're thinking about dating someone in your own program, be careful about power. Don't date anyone you supervise, teach or evaluate, and check your program's and hospital's policies first. Programs are small, and a breakup means you'll still share sign-out with them for years.

If you're a medical student and your partner is too, the NRMP Couples Match lets two applicants link their rank lists so they can try to match in the same place. It's worth reading up on early if you're headed that way.

Someone outside medicine

A partner with a normal job can be a real relief: someone who talks about anything except medicine, and a home that feels like a different world from the hospital. The cost is more explaining up front, and the risk that cancellations start to feel personal to them. That's where sharing your schedule early, and following through on what you do commit to, matters most.

If you're dating a resident

  • Ask about the rotation, not just the day. "What are you on this month?" tells you more than "are you free Friday?"
  • Plan around post-call. The day after a long call shift is for sleep. The day after that is usually the good one.
  • Don't keep score of cancellations. Watch whether they reschedule and show up when they can. That's the real signal.
  • Have your own life. Residency is a few years, not forever. People who keep their own friends and plans during it tend to resent it far less.

Look after yourself, too

Burnout and depression are common in residency, and loneliness makes both worse. If you've felt low for weeks, aren't sleeping even when you can, or feel numb about things you used to care about, tell someone: your own doctor, a counselor, or your program's wellness or employee assistance resources if it has them. In the US you can also call or text 988 to reach the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline at any hour.

Questions people ask

Is it possible to date during residency?

Yes. Plenty of residents date and plenty of relationships last through training. It takes more planning: say early that you're a resident, share your schedule once things are going well, and use your lighter rotations and full weekends off instead of trying to date post-call.

When is the best time to start dating in residency?

Usually during a lighter rotation, like an elective, clinic or research block. Starting something new in the middle of your hardest month is possible, but you'll have very little time to give it.

Is it a good idea to date a co-resident?

It can work, since they understand the hours, but programs are small and you'll keep working together if it ends. Never date someone you supervise or evaluate, and check your program's and hospital's policies first.

How do you date someone who is a resident?

Ask what rotation they're on, plan around post-call days, and judge them by whether they reschedule rather than by how often plans move. Keeping your own friends and routines makes the hard months much easier on both of you.
