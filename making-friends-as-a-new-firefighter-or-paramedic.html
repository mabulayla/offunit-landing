Friends for first responders

Making friends as a new firefighter or paramedic in a new town

You got the job, finished the academy or orientation, and moved somewhere new. Now you spend a third of your life with a crew and the rest of it in an apartment where you don't know anyone. Making friends as a new firefighter or paramedic takes some effort, but the schedule can actually work in your favor once you know how to use it.

Why it's harder than it looks

  • You moved for the job. Hiring processes are competitive, and a lot of people take the first good offer wherever it is. That often means a town where you know nobody.
  • Your crew is only one shift. On a rotating shift system you get to know your own shift well and barely meet the others. Your coworkers become close, but you're one group in the whole department.
  • Probation takes everything. Your first year is about learning, studying and proving you belong. There's not much social energy left over.
  • In EMS, partners change. You may ride with different people from shift to shift, and in busy systems turnover can be high. It's hard to build on that.
  • Your days off are weekdays. A 24-on, 48-off schedule sounds like a lot of free time, and it is. But the first day is often recovery, and the rest land when most people are at work.
  • Old friends don't get it. They mean well, but they don't know why you're asleep at 2pm or why you didn't want to talk about the call.

Start with the crew

In the fire service

The kitchen table is where the friendships start. Show up, do your share, and listen more than you talk while you're new. Ask the people who've been there longest where they eat, where they train and what they do on days off. Go to the things outside of shift when you're invited: a cookout, a fundraiser, a softball game. Most departments have more going on off duty than new people realize.

In EMS

Partner changes make it harder, so be the one who suggests breakfast after shift. Get to know the crews you keep running into at the hospital, the ER staff and the dispatchers whose voices you hear all day. EMS is a small world, and the same faces keep showing up.

Ask about the other shifts

If there's a department or company event that brings all the shifts together, go. It's the easiest way to meet people you'd otherwise never see.

Keep some friends outside the job

Your crew can become family, and that's one of the best parts of the work. But when every friend is on the job, a bad shift, an injury, a conflict at the station or a move to another department takes your whole social life with it. A few friends who don't wear a uniform give you somewhere to go that isn't work.

Things that tend to fit a rotation:

  • Gyms and classes with lots of time slots. Climbing gyms, martial arts and fitness classes with daytime options let you go whenever your days off fall.
  • Rec leagues that allow subs. Tell the captain you work 24s and will miss some weeks. Plenty of leagues are fine with that.
  • A regular spot. The same coffee shop, trail or dog park on your days off. Familiar faces turn into conversations.
  • Volunteering or a hobby group. People who show up to something on purpose are easier to get to know.

Pick one or two, not five. A routine you keep beats a plan you drop after two weeks.

Use the weekday advantage

You're free when most people are at work. So are a lot of other shift workers: nurses, police officers, dispatchers, other medics and firefighters from nearby departments. They're the people most likely to be around for a Tuesday hike or a Wednesday lunch, and they won't need your schedule explained.

Making plans on a rotation

  • Share your calendar early. Many fire rotations repeat on a fixed pattern, so you can tell a new friend which days you're off months ahead. That's a real advantage. Use it.
  • Suggest a specific time. "Climbing Thursday at 11?" works better than "we should hang out."
  • Avoid the morning after a bad night. If the shift was busy, plan for the next day instead.
  • Be the one who initiates for a while. Everyone is busy. The person who sends the second text usually ends up with the friends.
  • Reschedule in the same message. Held over, called in or swapped shifts? Offer a new time right away.

If it's more than lonely

Moving, a new job and the calls you're starting to run can add up. First responders deal with high rates of stress, depression and trauma, and isolation makes all of it worse. If you've felt low for weeks, aren't sleeping well even on days off, or keep replaying certain calls, talk to someone. Many departments and services have peer support teams, chaplains or an employee assistance program. In the US you can also call or text 988 any time.

Questions people ask

How do I make friends as a new firefighter?

Start with your crew: show up, do your share and go to the off-duty events you're invited to. Then add one or two regular things outside work, like a gym or a rec league that allows subs, so you see the same people on your days off. Other shift workers are often free at the same times you are.

How do paramedics make friends outside work?

Pick activities with flexible times, like gyms or classes with daytime slots, and use your weekdays off to meet other shift workers. Sharing your schedule early and suggesting specific times makes plans much more likely to happen.

Is it normal to feel lonely as a probationary firefighter or new medic?

Yes. A new job, a new town and a demanding first year all hit at once. It usually eases as you settle in, but if you've felt low for weeks or keep replaying calls, talk to your peer support team, employee assistance program or a doctor. In the US you can call or text 988 any time.

Should all my friends be on the job?

Friends on the job are great because they understand it. Keeping a few outside it means a bad stretch at work, an injury or a move doesn't take your whole social life with it.
